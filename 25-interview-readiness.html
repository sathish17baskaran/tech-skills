Section 25

Interview readiness for senior engineers and architects

Knowing the concepts is half the job. The other half is showing it under time pressure: telling clear stories, answering rapid technical questions, designing live and presenting a decision. This page turns the rest of the site into an interview plan. It covers the interview loop, the STAR method, a behavioural and a technical question bank, ten timed architecture katas, how to present a case study, and what to ask them.

All examples are generic and illustrative. Replace every [your example] with a real story from your own work, told without confidential details.

25.2 The STAR(R) method Added

Answer every "tell me about a time…" question as a short story: Situation, Task, Action, Result, plus a final Reflection on what you learned.

✅ Do

  • Aim for about 2 minutes, then stop and let them probe.
  • Say "I" for your actions. Use "we" only for the team result.
  • Quantify: "cut batch window from 6 h to 40 min", "zero P1s in 6 months".
  • Spend most of the time on Action: that's where your judgement shows.
  • Pick recent stories (last 3–5 years) at the level of the role.

❌ Common mistakes

  • Three minutes of Situation, ten seconds of Action.
  • "We decided…" with no clue what you did.
  • No result, or a vague one ("it went well").
  • A "failure" story where nothing actually failed.
  • Blaming others or sharing confidential details.

Fill-in template

PartPromptYour notes
SituationWhere, when, what system, why it mattered[your example]
TaskWhat was I accountable for? What was the constraint?[your example]
Action3 things I did; the options; the trade-off; who I influenced[your example]
ResultNumbers; business outcome; what changed for users[your example]
ReflectionWhat I learned; what I'd do differently[your example]
Themes it coversTag it so one story can answer several questionse.g. influence, migration, trade-off

Worked example illustrative, generic

"Tell me about a significant integration change you led." Migrating a nightly batch integration to events

  • Situation: A mid-sized lender sent customer and account changes from its core system to five downstream systems in one nightly file. Downstream data was up to 24 hours stale, and a failed file meant a manual re-run the next morning.
  • Task: As the integration architect, I owned the target design and had to move to near-real-time without a big-bang cutover or extra load on the core system.
  • Action: I compared three options: more frequent files, API polling, and change data capture into an event broker. I chose CDC with an outbox-style publisher, because it put no new query load on the core and gave ordered changes per account. I wrote the ADR, ran a workshop with the five consuming teams to agree an event contract, and planned a strangler-style rollout: one consumer at a time, with the batch file kept as a fallback and a daily reconciliation report. When the risk team worried about duplicates, I added idempotent consumers and showed them the reconciliation results.
  • Result: Over four months all five consumers moved. Data freshness went from up to 24 hours to under a minute, and manual re-runs stopped. The batch job was switched off after two clean months of parallel running.
  • Reflection: I'd involve the operations team earlier: we added replay tooling late, after their first support call.

Related pages: batch & file integration, CDC, strangler fig, idempotency, ADR.

The example above is invented to show the shape of an answer. Never present someone else's story as your own: interviewers probe, and a second question exposes it fast.

  • About 2 minutes. Most of it on Action, in the first person.
  • Every Result has a number or a clear before/after.
  • Add the Reflection: it shows self-awareness, which senior roles look for.
  • Keep 8–10 stories, each tagged with the themes it can answer.

Most interview stories fail in the Action part, not the Result ⏱️

🧩 Situation + Task: 30 seconds, no more

🛠️ Action: 60 seconds of what I did, including the trade-off

📈 Result: a number or a clear before/after

🔁 Reflection: what you'd do differently next time

STAR becomes STARR when you add the learning. That last R is what makes it sound senior.

25.3 Behavioural question bank for architects Added

Map your 8–10 stories against these themes. One good migration story can answer "leading", "trade-off", "ambiguity" and "influence" with a different emphasis each time. See also soft skills: trade-offs & stakeholders.

  • Find the hidden question: influence, ownership, judgement or integrity.
  • Failure stories need a real failure, your part in it, and what changed afterwards.
  • For security and compliance questions, the right answer never bypasses the control.

Every behavioural interview question has a hidden question behind it 🕵️

🗣️ "Changed direction without authority?" → can you influence with evidence?

💥 "A decision that went wrong?" → do you own mistakes and learn?

🚫 "Said no to the business?" → can you protect them and still help?

🔐 "Pressure to skip a control?" → do you have integrity under deadline?

Answer the hidden question, not just the spoken one. Which question catches you out?

25.4 Technical deep-dive questions Added

Rapid technical questions check breadth; the follow-ups check depth. Give a crisp one-line answer, then a trade-off, then an example.

QuestionOne-line model answerRead
CAP in one sentence?During a network partition you choose consistency or availability; PACELC adds latency vs consistency when there's no partition03 · PACELC
How does a quorum give consistency?If W + R > N, every read overlaps at least one node with the latest write04 · Quorum
2PC or saga across services?Saga: local transactions plus compensations; 2PC blocks and couples services04 · 2PC vs Saga
Why can't you trust timestamps for ordering?Clocks drift; use sequence numbers, logical clocks or a single partition per key04 · Time
Cache-aside vs write-through?Cache-aside loads on a miss (simple, may be stale); write-through updates cache with the DB (fresher, slower writes)05 · Strategies
What's a cache stampede and the fix?Many misses hit the DB at once when a hot key expires; use request coalescing, locks or jittered TTLs05 · Problems
When would you pick NoSQL over SQL?Known access patterns at large scale and flexible schema; SQL for joins, ad hoc queries and strong transactions06 · SQL vs NoSQL
Why use CDC instead of dual writes?CDC reads the DB log, so the event and the data can't disagree; dual writes can half-fail06 · CDC
How do you make a payment API safe to retry?An idempotency key stored atomically with the result; replay returns the same response08 · Idempotency
Token bucket vs sliding window?Token bucket allows bursts up to a limit; sliding window gives a smoother, more exact rate08 · Rate limits
OAuth vs OIDC?OAuth 2.0 delegates access (access token); OIDC adds identity on top (ID token)08 · OAuth/OIDC
What does an API gateway do that a load balancer doesn't?Auth, rate limits, transformation, versioning, analytics per API and consumer09 · Gateway vs LB
APIM versions vs revisions?Versions are breaking changes consumers choose; revisions are safe, non-breaking changes you roll out09 · Versions
Exactly-once delivery: real?Not end to end; use at-least-once delivery plus idempotent consumers (effectively once)10 · Guarantees
Queue vs event stream?A queue hands each message to one consumer; a stream keeps an ordered log many consumers replay10 · Queue vs Event
Orchestration vs choreography?A central coordinator owns the flow vs services reacting to events; orchestration is easier to see and change10 · Orch vs Chor
How do you replace a legacy system safely?Strangler fig: route slices of traffic to the new system behind a facade, one capability at a time11 · Strangler
mTLS: what does it add over TLS?Both sides present certificates, so the server also authenticates the client12 · TLS/mTLS
Zero Trust in one line?Never trust the network; verify identity, device and context on every request, with least privilege12 · Zero Trust
Blue-green vs canary?Blue-green switches all traffic at once with instant rollback; canary shifts a small percentage first and watches metrics13 · Deployment
Logs, metrics and traces: why all three?Metrics tell you something is wrong, traces show where, logs explain why13 · Observability
RPO vs RTO?RPO is how much data you can lose; RTO is how long you can be down03 · DR
RAG or fine-tuning?RAG for fresh or private facts with citations; fine-tuning for style, format or narrow skills14 · Approach
How do you evaluate a RAG system?Measure retrieval (recall, precision) and generation (faithfulness, relevance) on a golden set, in CI14 · RAG eval
How do you keep an AI agent safe?Least-privilege tools, input and output guardrails, human approval for risky actions, full tracing15 · Guardrails
async/await: does it make code faster?No; it frees threads while waiting on I/O, so a server handles more requests at once01 · Sync vs Async, 22

"I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" beats a confident guess. Interviewers probe until you hit your limit; they're testing honesty as much as knowledge.

  • Lead with one sentence. Stop. Let them pull you deeper.
  • Every answer is stronger with a trade-off and a "when I'd use it".
  • Know your own CV at depth: anything on it is fair game.

25.5 Architecture kata prompts Added

An architecture kata is a short, timed design exercise from a brief. You practise turning vague requirements into characteristics, components, trade-offs and a written decision.

Katas started as a workshop format by Ted Neward ("great architects only get to architect a handful of times in their career, so practise"). Do them alone with a timer, or with a friend playing the customer who answers your clarifying questions.

Requirementsusers, volumes, rules
→
Characteristicspick the top 3
→
ComponentsC4 L1–L2
→
Trade-offsoptions + why
→
ADRdecision + consequences

Each brief below is generic and simplified. Make and state your own assumptions about volumes and rules.

1. Real-time payments notification hub

Context: customers want a push, SMS or email within seconds of any incoming or outgoing payment, across real-time, card and batch channels.

Requirements: < 5 s for real-time payments; preferences and quiet hours; no duplicates; peaks of 10× on payday.

Hard decisions: one event model across channels; at-least-once plus dedupe; per-channel providers and fallback; what is safe to put in an SMS.

Read: 19 · Notification, 16 · NPP, 10 · Guarantees

2. Customer 360 with consent

Context: staff need one view of a customer across products, but data sits in many systems and use is limited by consent and purpose.

Requirements: near-real-time; consent and purpose enforced on read; audit of every view; mastered customer identity.

Hard decisions: virtual (API composition) vs materialised view; MDM matching; where consent is checked; retention and deletion.

Read: 07 · MDM, 07 · Privacy, 06 · CDC

3. Loan origination with document AI

Context: applicants upload payslips and statements; the lender wants faster decisions without losing control.

Requirements: extract fields with confidence scores; human review below a threshold; full audit trail; decision in hours, not days.

Hard decisions: workflow engine vs choreography; human-in-the-loop thresholds; storing documents securely; model risk and explainability.

Read: 20 · Onboarding, 15 · HITL, 06 · Storage

4. Fraud alerting

Context: score transactions in real time and alert customers and analysts on suspicious activity.

Requirements: score in the payment path within tight latency; rules plus ML; case management; feedback loop.

Hard decisions: inline (block) vs async (alert); fail-open vs fail-closed; feature store freshness; false-positive cost.

Read: 20 · Fraud, 03 · Resilience, 10 · Real-time

5. Open banking API platform

Context: expose standard data-sharing APIs to accredited third parties, with consent the customer can withdraw.

Requirements: strong security profile; consent dashboard; published availability and performance targets; versioned standards.

Hard decisions: gateway and auth server placement; consent store design; versioning as standards change; protecting core systems from load.

Read: 16 · CDR, 09 · APIM security, 08 · OAuth

6. Branch and ATM locator

Context: show nearby branches and ATMs with opening hours and live status in the app and website.

Requirements: fast "near me" search; status changes within minutes; high read volume; works when the core is down.

Hard decisions: geospatial index choice; CDN and cache strategy; source of truth for status; public vs authenticated API.

Read: 06 · Geospatial, 05 · CDN, 20 · Proximity

7. Regulatory reporting pipeline

Context: produce periodic regulatory returns from many source systems, with reconciliation and sign-off.

Requirements: lineage from report cell to source; repeatable runs; late data handling; segregation of duties.

Hard decisions: ETL vs ELT; lakehouse layering; data quality gates; how to restate a past period.

Read: 06 · Lakehouse, 07 · Lineage, 10 · Batch

8. GenAI assistant for contact-centre agents

Context: agents need fast, correct answers from policy and product documents while on a call.

Requirements: cited answers; no customer data leaking into prompts or logs; answers in seconds; measurable quality.

Hard decisions: RAG design and chunking; permission-aware retrieval; evaluation and guardrails; build on a platform vs buy.

Read: 14 · RAG, 14 · Responsible AI, 20 · RAG assistant

9. Core banking event streaming

Context: publish account and transaction events from a legacy core so new digital services stop polling it.

Requirements: ordered per account; replay; schema evolution; no extra load on the core.

Hard decisions: CDC vs outbox vs core adapters; partition keys; event schema governance; canonical vs domain events.

Read: 16 · Core & ledger, 06 · CDC, 10 · Events

10. Multi-region disaster recovery

Context: a critical customer-facing service must survive a region loss within agreed tolerance levels (as APRA CPS 230 expects for critical operations).

Requirements: RTO and RPO from tolerance levels; tested failover; data residency in Australia; cost-aware.

Hard decisions: active-active vs active-passive; data replication mode; DNS or global load balancer failover; how often to test.

Read: 03 · DR, 03 · Multi-region, 16 · Regulation

Finish every kata with a one-page ADR (17 · ADR) and a list of risks. Then compare with the design review checklist and note what you missed.

  • Time-box each stage. Running out of time before trade-offs is the most common kata failure.
  • Pick the top 3 characteristics. "Everything is important" means nothing is.
  • End with a written decision: an ADR shows you can commit, not just list options.

Architects only design a few big systems in a career. So how do you practise? Katas 🥋

⏱️ 60 minutes, one brief, a timer

🎯 Clarify requirements, then pick the top 3 quality attributes

🧱 Sketch the context and containers

⚖️ Spend real time on 3–4 hard decisions

📝 Finish with an ADR

Try one this week: "real-time payment notifications in under 5 seconds". What's your first hard decision?

25.6 Presenting an architecture or case study Added

A case presentation tests whether you can tell a decision story: the problem, the constraints, the options, what you chose and why, and what could go wrong.

Drawing on a whiteboard: zoom in with C4

See the C4 model for notation. Label every arrow with what flows and whether it is sync or async.

Handling challenge questions

🎧 Listen fully

Let them finish. Restate the question: "So the concern is failover time?"

✅ Agree with what's true

"Yes, that's a real risk." Defensiveness costs more than the weakness.

⚖️ Explain the trade-off

"We accepted X because Y mattered more, and we mitigate with Z."

🔁 Show you'd adapt

"If volumes grew 10×, I'd revisit by doing…" Shows the decision isn't dogma.

One-page decision memo template

HeadingWhat to write (1–3 lines each)
DecisionOne sentence: "We will…"
Context & problemWhy now; business impact; who is affected
ConstraintsRegulation, budget, deadline, skills, existing platforms
Options considered2–3 options with one pro and one con each
Recommendation & rationaleThe option, the criteria it wins on, what we give up
Risks & mitigationsTop risks, owner, mitigation
Cost & roadmapRough cost; phases; first milestone
AskWhat you need: approval, funding, people, a decision by a date
  • Problem first, technology last. Panels remember the "why".
  • Show at least two real options. One option is a sales pitch, not a decision.
  • Name your risks before they do: it builds trust.
  • Rehearse to time, out loud, at least twice.

The best architecture presentations spend more time on "why" than "what" 🧭

❓ Problem: what hurts, and for whom

🚧 Constraints: regulation, budget, deadline

⚖️ Options: at least two, honestly compared

✅ Decision: what you chose and what you gave up

⚠️ Risks: name them before the panel does

Defending a design is easier when the trade-off is already on the slide. How do you structure yours?

25.7 Questions to ask them & final checklist Added

Your questions show how you think about the role. A 30-60-90 day plan shows you can land well. A checklist stops avoidable mistakes on the day.

Smart questions to ask

🏛️ Architecture governance

  • How are architecture decisions made and recorded here?
  • What does a design review look like, and who attends?

🧱 Tech debt

  • What's the biggest piece of tech debt slowing delivery today?
  • How is fixing it funded against new features?

👥 Team topology

  • Are architects embedded in teams or in a central function?
  • Who owns platforms like the API gateway and event broker?

🚚 Delivery model

  • How does work flow from idea to production?
  • How often do you release, and what slows it down?

🎯 Success

  • What would success look like in this role after 90 days? After a year?
  • What did the last person in this role do well, or struggle with?

⚠️ Risk & change

  • Which regulatory or resilience programmes affect this team most?
  • Is there anything in my background that worries you for this role?

30-60-90 day plan outline

The day before

Keep one page of notes next to you in a video interview: your story list, key numbers and your questions. Glance, don't read.

  • Ask about how decisions are made, what success looks like, and the biggest problem they need solved.
  • A 30-60-90 plan is learn, contribute, lead. Don't promise to redesign everything in month one.
  • Prepare logistics the day before, so your energy goes into the conversation.

⚡ Quick-fire Q&A

How long should a STAR answer be?

About two minutes. Spend most of it on the Action, in the first person, end with a measurable Result and a short Reflection, then stop and let the interviewer ask follow-ups.

How many stories should I prepare?

Eight to ten strong stories, each tagged with the themes it covers (influence, conflict, failure, trade-off, migration, incident, mentoring, ambiguity). One story can answer several questions with a different focus.

What if I don't know the answer to a technical question?

Say so, then reason out loud: what you do know, how you'd find out, and what you'd check. Honesty plus a structured approach scores better than a confident wrong answer.

What is an architecture kata?

A short, timed design exercise from a brief: clarify requirements, choose the top quality attributes, sketch components, discuss trade-offs, and record a decision. The format started as a workshop by Ted Neward.

How should I structure a 25-minute architecture presentation?

Problem and goals, constraints and NFRs, two or three options, the decision and design (C4 context and containers), risks and mitigations, roadmap and outcome, then a short summary. Leave time for challenge questions.

What should a 30-60-90 day plan for an architect show?

Learn (stakeholders, systems, pain points) in the first 30 days, contribute (a quick win, design reviews, a first ADR) by 60, and lead (an agreed target state and roadmap) by 90.
