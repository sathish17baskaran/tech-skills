7.1 Data governance basics Added

Data governance is the set of roles, policies and processes that decide who owns data, who may use it, and how it must be handled through its whole life.

👔 Data owner

A senior business person who is accountable for a data domain (e.g. "Customer"). Approves access, and sets the classification and retention.

📖 Data steward

A business expert who looks after the data day to day: definitions, quality rules and glossary terms. Fixes quality issues.

🛠️ Data custodian

Usually IT or the platform team. Stores, secures, backs up and moves the data, and applies the controls the owner asked for.

👩‍💻 Data consumer

Anyone who uses the data: analysts, apps, models. Uses it only for the approved purpose.

🏛️ Governance council / CDO

Sets the policies, settles disputes ("whose definition of 'active customer' wins?") and tracks maturity.

  • Why architects care: every design decision touches it. Where data lands, who can read it, how long it stays, and what happens when a field changes.
  • Put governance into the platform ("governance as code"): tags, policies and pipelines that enforce the rules, not a wiki page people forget.
  • The owner is the business, not IT. IT is the custodian.
  • Good governance makes data easier to use safely. If it only says "no", people go around it.
  • In Australian banking, APRA's CPG 235 Managing Data Risk describes the same owner / steward / custodian roles.

7.2 Data classification Added

Classification labels data by how much harm its exposure would cause, so every dataset automatically gets the right controls.

🟢 Public

Safe to publish. Interest rates on the website, product brochures.

🔵 Internal

For staff only. Low harm if leaked. Org charts, internal procedures.

🟠 Confidential

Harm to customers or the business if leaked. Customer names, contact details, balances, contracts.

🔴 Restricted

Serious harm. Card numbers, TFNs, passwords, health or hardship notes, bulk customer extracts.

Names vary (some use "Highly confidential" or "Secret"), but four levels like these are typical.

Personal information (PII)

Under the Privacy Act: information or an opinion about an identified or reasonably identifiable person. Name, email, address, phone, account number, and often device IDs or IP addresses when they can be linked to a person.

Sensitive information

A special Privacy Act category with a higher bar (usually needs consent): health, genetic and biometric data, racial or ethnic origin, religion, political opinions, sexual orientation, criminal record and more. Some data has its own rules too: card numbers (PCI DSS) and TFNs (the TFN Rule).

  • Labelling: tag columns in the catalogue, put sensitivity labels on documents and emails (e.g. Microsoft Purview Information Protection), and tag cloud resources so policies can act on them.
  • Automate discovery: scanners spot patterns like card numbers, TFNs and emails in files and tables you didn't know held them.
  • A dataset takes the level of its most sensitive field.
  • Aggregation raises the level: one customer's postcode is low risk; a million names with postcodes and balances is not.
  • APRA CPS 234 requires regulated entities to classify information assets by criticality and sensitivity, and to match controls to that classification.
  • The cheapest control is not collecting the field at all.

🟢 Public: rates on the website

🔵 Internal: staff procedures

🟠 Confidential: customer contact details, balances

🔴 Restricted: card numbers, TFNs, health or hardship notes

The label drives encryption, access, masking, logging and sharing. Two traps: a dataset takes its most sensitive field's level, and combining "harmless" data can make it sensitive.

7.3 Privacy & PII handling Added

Privacy means collecting only what you need, using it only in ways people would expect, protecting it end to end, and respecting people's rights over it.

🧱 Privacy by design

Build privacy in from the first design, not as a fix later. Default to the most private setting.

✂️ Data minimisation

Collect and keep the smallest set of fields that does the job. Data you don't hold can't leak.

🎯 Purpose limitation

Use data for the purpose it was collected for, or a related one people would reasonably expect.

✅ Consent & notice

Tell people what you collect and why. Get consent where the law requires it (e.g. sensitive information).

🙋 Individual rights

People can ask to access and correct their data. Some laws add erasure and portability.

🔒 Security

Reasonable technical and organisational steps to protect it. See 12.5 and 12.7.

Pseudonymised data is still personal data.

Australia has no general "right to be forgotten" today. But APP 11.2 already requires you to destroy or de-identify personal information you no longer need, so you need deletion pipelines anyway (see 7.5).

  • Pseudonymised ≠ anonymised. If anyone can re-link it, privacy law still applies.
  • Run a privacy impact assessment (PIA) early for any new use of personal data, especially AI and analytics.
  • Keep legal calls with privacy and legal teams. The architect's job is to make minimisation, masking, access and deletion easy to do.

If you lose the Data Protection key ring, anything you stored with it can never be decrypted. Persist it, back it up, and never delete old keys while data still uses them.

Masked, tokenised, pseudonymised, anonymised. They are NOT the same thing 🕵️

🎭 Masked: "•••• 1234" on screen. The real value is still stored.

🔐 Tokenised: a random token; only the vault can swap it back.

🔑 Pseudonymised: a keyed ID. Re-linkable, so still personal information.

🌫️ Anonymised: can't be linked back to anyone. Prove it before you claim it.

And in Australia: from 10 December 2026, privacy policies must explain automated decisions that significantly affect people. Does yours?

7.4 Data residency & sovereignty Added

Residency is where data physically sits. Sovereignty is whose laws can reach it. Localisation is a rule that says it must stay in a country.

📍 Residency

"Our data is stored in Australia East and Australia Southeast." A fact about location, usually chosen for regulation, contracts or latency.

⚖️ Sovereignty

Which governments can compel access. A foreign-owned provider may be subject to its home country's laws even for data stored in Australia.

🚧 Localisation

A legal or contract rule that data must not leave a country. Some government and defence work has this.

  • APP 8 (cross-border disclosure): before disclosing personal information to someone overseas, take reasonable steps so they don't breach the APPs. In most cases you stay accountable for what they do with it.
  • OAIC guidance says that, in limited cases, giving data to an overseas cloud provider that only stores it and can't access it may be a "use", not a "disclosure". Get privacy advice for your case.
  • APRA context: CPS 234 (information security, including data held by third parties), CPS 230 (operational risk and material service providers, in force from 1 July 2025) and CPG 235 (data risk) all apply when a bank puts data with a cloud provider or offshore.

  • Map every copy: primary, replicas, backups, logs, analytics, AI prompts and support access.
  • Enforce location with platform policy, not just app settings.

7.5 Data retention & deletion Added

Keep data as long as the law and the business need it, and no longer. Then delete it everywhere, including the copies.

Data (Australian examples)Keep forDriver
AML/CTF transaction records7 years from when the record is madeAML/CTF Act (AUSTRAC)
AML/CTF customer due diligence (KYC) records7 years after the customer relationship endsAML/CTF Act (AUSTRAC)
Company financial records7 years after the transactionsCorporations Act
Personal information you no longer needDestroy or de-identifyPrivacy Act, APP 11.2
App and security logsYour policy (e.g. 90 days hot, 1 year archive)Security monitoring and investigations

A retention schedule lists every data type, how long to keep it, why, and what happens at the end. When several rules apply to the same record, keep it for the longest period that applies, and no longer.

⚖️ Legal hold

Litigation, a regulator or an investigation can freeze deletion for specific records. A hold overrides the schedule until it is released.

🗂️ Soft vs hard delete

Soft delete (an IsDeleted flag) is handy for undo. It is not deletion for privacy or retention. Plan the hard delete too.

💾 Backups

You usually can't edit backups. Let them age out on a short, documented cycle, and keep a deletion log that is replayed after any restore.

📜 Logs

Keep personal information out of logs in the first place (see 7.2 redaction). Then log retention is easy.

🔑 Crypto-shredding

Encrypt each customer's (or each period's) data with its own key. Delete the key, and every copy, including backups, becomes unreadable. Key management: 12.7.

  • Over-retention is a risk, not a safety net: old data is breach exposure with no business value.
  • Deletion must reach every copy: replicas, history tables, lake files, extracts, caches, backups and search indexes.
  • AML/CTF records are generally kept for 7 years; check the exact trigger (record made vs relationship ended).

{
    b.Entity<Customer>(e =>
    {

// Audit question: "what did we hold for this customer on 31 January?"

Temporal history tables and Key Vault soft-delete both keep data on purpose. Your erasure process must purge history rows too, and purge protection on a vault means a deleted key can't be purged until its retention period ends.

Deleting a customer's data is harder than storing it 🗑️

📋 Start with a retention schedule: what, how long, why

⚖️ Legal hold beats the schedule until it's released

💾 Backups: short cycles + a deletion log replayed after restore

🔑 Crypto-shredding: one key per customer; delete the key and every copy is unreadable

Old data you don't need isn't an asset. It's breach exposure. When did you last delete anything on purpose?

7.6 Data quality & lineage Added

Data quality measures whether data is fit for its purpose. Lineage shows where data came from, how it changed, and where it goes.

🎯 Accuracy

Matches reality. The address on file is where the customer actually lives.

🧩 Completeness

Required values are present. No customers without a date of birth.

🔗 Consistency

Agrees across systems. The balance in the ledger equals the balance in the data warehouse.

⏱️ Timeliness

Fresh enough for its use. The fraud feed lands within 5 minutes.

✅ Validity

Follows format and rules. A BSB has 6 digits; amounts are positive.

1️⃣ Uniqueness

No duplicates. One record per customer (see 7.7).

  • Why lineage matters: impact analysis ("if I change this column, what breaks?"), root cause ("why is this number wrong?"), and regulators. BCBS 239 expects banks to trace risk figures back to their source.
  • Table vs column lineage: table-level is easy to collect; column-level is what auditors and impact analysis really need.
  • OpenLineage is an open standard for lineage events (job, run, input and output datasets). Spark, Airflow and dbt integrations emit it; Marquez is the reference backend.
  • Microsoft Purview scans sources, builds the catalogue, and collects lineage from services such as Azure Data Factory and Synapse.
  • Quality tools outside .NET: Great Expectations, Soda and dbt tests run checks inside data pipelines.
  • Fix quality at the source and as close to capture as possible. Cleaning downstream forever is the expensive option.
  • Measure quality as numbers with thresholds and owners, not as opinions.
  • Quarantine and alert. Don't silently drop bad rows or load them anyway.

"The numbers in the report are wrong." The 3 questions that decide how bad your week is 📉

1️⃣ Which quality check should have caught it? (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness)

2️⃣ Where did the bad data come from? That's lineage.

3️⃣ What else uses it? That's lineage too.

🚧 Quality gates + quarantine in every pipeline. Never fail silently.

Trustworthy data is a design choice, not luck.

7.7 Master data management (MDM) Added

MDM creates one trusted golden record for core business entities, such as customer and product, that many systems share.

👤 Master data

The core "nouns" of the business, shared by many systems: customer, product, account, supplier, employee.

📚 Reference data

Standard code lists: country codes, currencies (ISO 4217), BSB directory, merchant category codes. Small, slow-changing, often external.

💳 Transactional data

Events that involve master data: payments, orders. High volume. Not what MDM manages.

MDM styleHow it worksGood forTrade-off
RegistrySources keep their data. The hub holds only match links and IDs, and builds the view on demandFast start, no change to source systemsSources stay messy; the view is read-only
ConsolidationCopy data into the hub, match and merge into golden records for analyticsReporting and a single customer viewGolden record doesn't flow back to sources
CoexistenceLike consolidation, but the golden record is synced back to sourcesGradual move to clean data everywhereTwo-way sync and conflict rules are complex
Centralised (transaction)The hub is the system of record. New data is created and changed only thereHighest quality and controlMost invasive and costly; every app must change
  • MDM is mostly process and stewardship, then tooling. Agree who owns each field first.
  • Tune match thresholds with stewards: false merges (two people merged into one) are worse than missed matches in banking.
  • Start with registry or consolidation; go centralised only when the business case is clear.

Your bank has 3 versions of you. MDM picks the truth 👤

🏦 Core banking: verified date of birth

7.8 Data contracts & data mesh Added

A data contract is a versioned promise from a data producer to its consumers. Data mesh is an operating model where business domains own and publish data as products, under shared rules.

📐 Schema

Fields, types, required or optional. Avro, Protobuf, JSON Schema or OpenAPI.

📖 Semantics

What each field means: "amount is in cents, AUD, including GST".

⏱️ Quality & SLAs

Freshness, completeness and availability targets, such as "lands by 6 am, at most 0.5% nulls".

👤 Ownership

Owning team, support channel, classification (see 7.2).

🔢 Versioning

Backward-compatible changes in place; breaking changes get a new major version and a deprecation window.

1. Domain ownership

The team that runs the business process (payments, lending) owns its analytical data too. No central team as a bottleneck.

2. Data as a product

Each dataset is a product with an owner, documentation, a contract, SLAs and users it must keep happy.

3. Self-serve platform

A platform team provides storage, pipelines, catalogue and access control as self-service, so domains don't rebuild plumbing.

4. Federated governance

Shared global rules (classification, privacy, interoperability) set together and automated in the platform; domains decide the rest.

✅ Mesh fits when

Many domains, many data producers, a central data team that is a bottleneck, and strong platform engineering.

❌ Mesh is overkill when

A small organisation, a handful of sources, a central team that keeps up, or domains without the skills to own data products. Data contracts alone still help.

Data fabric, in one line: a technology-led approach that uses metadata, a catalogue and automation to connect data across sources. Mesh is mainly about organisation; the two can work together.

  • Start with contracts on your most important feeds. You don't need a full mesh to get most of the benefit.
  • Enforce contracts in CI and at the registry. A contract in a wiki is just a hope.
  • Mesh changes the org chart before it changes the tech.

using var producer = new ProducerBuilder<string, PaymentEvent>(producerConfig)
    .SetValueSerializer(new AvroSerializer<PaymentEvent>(registry, serdeConfig))
    .Build();

await producer.ProduceAsync("payments.v1",
    new Message<string, PaymentEvent> { Key = payment.AccountId, Value = payment });

PaymentEvent here is a class generated from the Avro schema (e.g. with avrogen). The schema file, not the C# class, is the contract.

Most "data quality issues" are really broken promises between teams 🤝

📐 Data contract: schema + meaning + SLAs + owner + version

🚫 Schema registry: breaking changes fail the build, not the dashboard

🧭 Data mesh: domains own data as products, on a self-serve platform, with federated governance

⚠️ Small org, few sources? Mesh is overkill. Contracts alone get you most of the value.

⚡ Quick-fire Q&A

Masking vs tokenisation vs pseudonymisation vs anonymisation?

Masking hides part of the value on display; the real value is still stored. Tokenisation swaps the value for a random token that only a vault can reverse. Pseudonymisation replaces identifiers with a keyed ID; it can be re-linked, so it is still personal information. Anonymisation can't be linked back to a person, so privacy law generally stops applying, but you must test the re-identification risk.

How do you delete a customer's data when it's also in backups?

Hard-delete it in live systems (including history tables, lake files and indexes), keep a deletion log that is replayed after any restore, and let backups age out on a short, documented cycle. For a stronger guarantee, use crypto-shredding: encrypt each customer's data with its own key and delete the key. Always check legal holds and AML/CTF retention first.

We host in Australia East. Is that enough for data residency?

Not by itself. Check every copy (replicas, backups, logs, CDN, AI endpoints), keep keys in an Australian vault, block other regions with policy, and control offshore support access, which can count as a cross-border disclosure under APP 8. Residency also isn't sovereignty: the provider's home-country laws may still reach the data.

When would you not recommend a data mesh?

In small or medium organisations with few sources, a central data team that keeps up, or domain teams without data engineering skills or a self-serve platform. Start with data contracts, a catalogue and clear ownership. Move to mesh when the central team becomes the bottleneck.
