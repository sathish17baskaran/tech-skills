Titles vary. A solution architect in one company is an enterprise architect in another (who works on portfolio and capability, not one solution). Some companies rank staff above principal, others the reverse. Read the job description, not the title.

Which role are you interviewing for? Tune your answers

🏛️ Solution Architect

Lead with business outcome, options and trade-offs. Name NFRs, risk and the ADR you wrote.

📐 Solution Designer

Show requirements to design: interfaces, data mapping, edge cases and a clean handover.

👥 Engineering Manager

  • Architect owns the shape, designer the detail, manager the team, principal the technical bar.
  • Higher altitude means a longer horizon and more influence without authority.

  • Tune every story to the role's primary output.

Four roles. Same project. Very different jobs 👇

🏛️ Solution Architect: decides the shape. Options, trade-offs, ADRs.

📐 Solution Designer: makes it buildable. Interfaces, mappings, edge cases.

👥 Engineering Manager: builds the team that builds it. People, flow, delivery.

🧭 Principal Engineer: sets the technical bar across teams, and still codes.

The difference is altitude and time horizon, not seniority. And titles vary by company, so read the job description first.

29.2 Solution Architect foundations Added

A solution architect turns business strategy into a target state, a roadmap and guardrails that many teams can deliver safely. They own the cross-cutting decisions, not the code.

1 · The role and its altitude

AltitudeHorizonCore question
Portfolio / solution, across many teams6–36 months"Are we building the right thing, the right way, for the business?"

2 · What you own

🧭 Responsibilities

Target state and roadmap, NFRs, risk, standards, alignment of teams and vendors.

⚖️ Decisions

Build / buy / reuse, cloud and integration strategy, patterns, AI use-case approval.

📄 Artefacts

HLD, ADRs, C4 context views, roadmaps, risk register.

  • ISO/IEC 25010 quality attributes; Well-Architected pillars; ATAM for trade-off reviews.

  • Reuse: fewer duplicate platforms, legacy retired.
  • NFRs met in production; few design-gap incidents.
  • Decision lead time; roadmap delivery; run cost vs forecast.

7 · What the interview tests

Rounds: hiring manager, design kata, case presentation, stakeholder panel and behavioural.

How to answer "What does a good solution architect do?"

  1. Start with the business outcome, not technology.
  2. Name the altitude: many teams, a multi-year horizon.
  3. Three verbs: shape the target, decide trade-offs, guard NFRs and risk.
  4. Give one story with a metric.

8 · First 30-60-90 days

9 · Pitfalls interviewers notice

  • Ivory tower: diagrams with no run cost.
  • Tech first: products before the problem.
  • Governance as a gate, not a paved road.
  • Too deep: designing every interface (the designer's job).
  • No exit plan for vendors or AI models.
  • Altitude is portfolio: many teams, multi-year.
  • Own the target state, roadmap, NFRs and risk, not the code.
  • Every big call gets an ADR with rejected options.
  • Influence comes from evidence and trust, not authority.
  • Treat AI as a governed capability: use-case gate, evaluation, exit plan.

A solution architect isn't the person who draws the best boxes 🧭

🎯 They start from the business outcome, not the product catalogue

⚖️ They make trade-offs visible: options, criteria, an ADR

🛡️ They turn "fast and secure" into numbers: RTO, p99, error budgets

🛣️ They build paved roads so governance feels like help

🤝 They lead without authority, through evidence and trust

Good architecture is a set of decisions that still make sense in three years. Which of yours would pass that test?

How to answer "What does a good solution designer do?"

  1. Contrast with the architect: depth, not breadth.
  2. Walk the chain: requirement → HLD → LLD → test.
  3. Stress the unhappy paths: errors, retries, security.
  4. Prove it with a story: fewer defects or faster build.

8 · First 30-60-90 days

9 · Pitfalls interviewers notice

  • Happy path only: no timeouts or compensation.
  • Vague contracts: "returns customer data", no schema.
  • Strategy talk: an architect's answer to a designer question.
  • Designing alone, then throwing it over the wall.
  • Altitude is one solution, in depth.
  • Architect = breadth and strategy; designer = depth and detail.
  • Own contracts, data mappings, sequences and error handling.
  • Design the unhappy paths first.
  • Every requirement traces to a design element and a test.

A design is finished when a developer can build it without guessing 🔬

📋 Every requirement traces to a component and a test

📜 Every API has a contract: schema, errors, versioning, idempotency

🗺️ Every field has a source, a rule and an edge case

💥 Every call has a timeout, a retry and a plan for failure

🤝 BAs, testers and developers shape it with you

Architects decide where we're going. Designers make sure we get there. What's the first unhappy path you design?

29.4 Engineering Manager foundations Added

An Engineering Manager builds a team that delivers: the right people, healthy ways of working and predictable, reliable outcomes. You succeed through the team, not your own commits.

1 · The role and its altitude

🎯 One line

Hire, grow and lead engineers who ship the right things, sustainably.

🧭 Altitude

Team(s) and people: 1–3 teams, 5–15 engineers.

⏳ Horizon

This sprint to 2–4 quarters.

2 · What you own

📋 Responsibilities

  • Hiring, onboarding
  • 1:1s, feedback, performance
  • Career growth, team health
  • Delivery, incidents
  • AI-assisted engineering

⚖️ Decisions

  • Capacity split: features, debt, ops
  • Performance, promotions
  • When to say no

📄 Artefacts

  • Team charter
  • OKRs, roadmap
  • Capacity plan, budget
  • Hiring plan, growth plans

6 · How you are measured

  • Reliability: SLOs met, on-call load.
  • People: regretted attrition, engagement, promotions.

Rounds: hiring manager, people behavioural, delivery, a lighter system design, a product or peer panel.

🗣️ "What does a good EM do?"

  1. One line: builds a team that delivers sustainably.
  2. Pillars: people, delivery, operations.
  3. One practice per pillar.
  4. Measures: DORA, SPACE, retention.
  5. A story with a number.

8 · First 30-60-90 days

9 · Pitfalls interviewers notice

  • Hero coder: the team waits on you.
  • Metrics as targets: ranking teams on DORA.
  • Surprise feedback at review time.
  • AI tools mandated without guardrails.
  • Altitude: team and people. The team's output is yours.
  • Pillars: people, delivery, operational health.
  • Use DORA + SPACE as team trends, never to rank people.
  • Match style to person and task (situational leadership).
  • Share bad news early; protect capacity for debt and ops.

The best engineering managers I've seen write the least code on the team. On purpose. 👇

👥 People: weekly 1:1s, specific feedback, no surprises at review time

🚀 Delivery: small batches, forecasts as ranges, honest trade-offs with stakeholders

🛟 Operations: SLOs, blameless postmortems, an on-call rota people can live with

📏 Measures: DORA and SPACE trends for the team, never a leaderboard of individuals

🤖 AI tools: pilot, measure, add guardrails, then scale

Your output is the team's output. What did your best manager do that others didn't?

29.6 How the four roles work together Added

One initiative passes through all four roles: the architect shapes it, the designer details it, the principal makes it technically sound, and the manager gets a team to deliver and run it. Clear handoffs and artefacts stop work falling between them.

RACI across the lifecycle

R does the work · A owns the outcome (one per row) · C consulted · I informed. The product or business owner is accountable for the business case.

StageArchitectDesignerEng MgrPrincipal
Idea / business caseR (feasibility)IC (capacity)C
Architecture & optionsA / RCCR (spikes)
Detailed designC (conformance)A / RCC
BuildIC (queries)AR (with team)
Test / releaseICAR
Run / incidentIIAR (deep fixes)
ImproveA (target state)CR (team practices)R (tech debt)

RACI varies by company. In an interview, say who you'd confirm it with in your first weeks, not that it's fixed.

Friction points and how strong candidates handle them

FrictionRoot causeStrong move
Architect vs principal on tech choiceStandards vs local fitWrite both options in an ADR against agreed NFRs; time-boxed spike decides
Designer vs manager on scope and datesDesign detail vs capacitySlice scope into a thin first release; make the trade-off visible to the product owner
Manager vs architect on standards vs autonomyConsistency vs team speedPaved road plus guardrails; exceptions via ADR, not by silence

Handoff artefacts

Architect → all

ACD, HLD, NFRs

Designer → team

LLD: interfaces, mappings, sequences

Anyone → record

ADRs: decision, options, consequences

Team → operations

Runbooks and alerts before go-live

  • Architect shapes, designer details, principal de-risks, manager delivers and runs.
  • One A per stage; ambiguity about who decides causes most friction.
  • Settle technical disagreements with an ADR and evidence, not seniority.
  • Fix scope-vs-date tension by slicing scope, not by cutting quality.
  • Handoffs are conversations backed by artefacts, not documents thrown over a wall.

Most delivery pain isn't technical. It's a handoff between roles 👇

🏛️ Architect to designer: an HLD with no NFRs means the designer guesses.

📐 Designer to manager: an LLD with no slicing means one huge, late release.

🧭 Principal vs architect: settle tech choices with an ADR and a spike, not rank.

👥 Manager vs architect: paved roads beat both mandates and free-for-alls.

Agree one accountable owner per stage, and write decisions down.

⚡ Quick-fire Q&A

How is a solution architect different from a principal engineer?

The architect works at solution altitude (options, NFRs, build vs buy, ADRs) across business and risk stakeholders. The principal works from the codebase up: technical approach, quality bar and cross-team standards, and still writes code.

How is a solution designer different from a solution architect?

The architect decides the shape of the solution and its trade-offs; the designer turns that into buildable detail: interfaces, data mappings, sequences and edge cases in an LLD.

What does a solution architect own that a delivery team doesn't?

The cross-team decisions: target state, roadmap, NFRs, build/buy/reuse, integration and cloud strategy, and the risks between systems. Teams own how they build inside those guardrails.

How does a solution architect lead without authority?

Through evidence and trust: involve teams early, show options and criteria in an ADR, prove ideas with a spike, and make the standard the easiest path (a paved road).

Solution architect vs solution designer: where is the line?

The architect works in breadth (many teams, strategy, target state). The designer works in depth on one solution: LLD, API contracts, data mappings, sequences and error handling.

When is a low-level design "done"?

When a developer can build it and a tester can test it without guessing: contracts and errors are specified, every field is mapped, failure paths are designed and every requirement traces to a test.

How should an Engineering Manager use DORA and SPACE?

As team-level trends for improvement, read together: DORA for delivery speed and stability, SPACE for satisfaction, collaboration and flow. Never rank teams or judge individuals on them, or they get gamed.

What is situational leadership?

Hersey and Blanchard's model: adapt your style (directing, coaching, supporting, delegating) to a person's competence and commitment on a specific task. A strong senior may need delegating on design but coaching on stakeholder work.

What are the staff engineer archetypes?

From Will Larson's book Staff Engineer: Tech Lead (guides one team's execution), Architect (owns direction for a critical area), Solver (goes deep on the hardest problems) and Right Hand (extends a senior leader's reach across the org).

How does a principal engineer differ from an architect and an EM?

The principal stays hands-on in the code and systems across teams. The architect owns the wider solution or enterprise picture and governance. The EM owns people and delivery. The principal partners with both and leads through RFCs, reviews and prototypes.

A principal engineer disagrees with your architecture standard. What do you do?

Write both options in an ADR against the agreed NFRs, run a time-boxed spike if evidence is missing, decide, and record the consequences. Allow an exception if local fit justifies it.

Who is accountable during build and run?

Usually the engineering manager is accountable; the principal and team are responsible for the work, and the architect and designer are consulted or informed. Confirm the local RACI early.
